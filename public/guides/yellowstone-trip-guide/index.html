Yellowstone: how to plan a first trip to the first national park

Yellowstone is the trip so many American families took at least once: the station wagon packed to the roof, a cooler of sandwiches, and a whole carful of people holding their breath on the benches at Old Faithful, waiting, waiting, until the first burst of steam and the cheer that went up from the crowd. Generations later, people still gather on those same benches, still cheer, and still drive home with a hundred photos of bison.

Yellowstone was established in 1872 as the first national park in the world. Most of it lies in the northwest corner of Wyoming, with edges in Montana and Idaho. It is big, around 2.2 million acres, so a little planning goes a long way.

How the park is laid out

A figure-eight road called the Grand Loop connects most of the famous sights. Five entrances lead into it. From Utah, the West Entrance at West Yellowstone, Montana, is usually the most convenient, and it is close to the geyser basins. Driving the whole loop takes much longer than the map suggests because of speed limits, wildlife jams and stops, so plan one or two areas per day.

Geysers and hot springs

  • Old Faithful: the most famous geyser in the world, erupting on a fairly predictable schedule. Rangers post predicted times at the visitor center and on the park app.
  • Upper Geyser Basin: the boardwalks around Old Faithful lead past dozens of other geysers and pools, including colorful Morning Glory Pool.
  • Grand Prismatic Spring: the huge rainbow-colored hot spring in Midway Geyser Basin. For the classic view from above, walk the Fairy Falls trail to the overlook.
  • Norris Geyser Basin: the hottest and most changeable thermal area in the park, with a stark, otherworldly look.

Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone

Yes, Yellowstone has its own grand canyon. The Yellowstone River cuts through yellow and pink rock and plunges over the Upper and Lower Falls. The Lower Falls is more than 300 feet tall. Artist Point and Lookout Point on the rims give the best views.

Wildlife

The Lamar Valley in the northeast is often called America's Serengeti. Early morning and evening are the best times to spot bison, elk, pronghorn and, with binoculars and luck, wolves and bears. Hayden Valley, between the canyon and Yellowstone Lake, is another good place to watch bison herds cross the road.

Keep your distance. The park asks visitors to stay at least 100 yards from bears and wolves and at least 25 yards from all other animals, including bison, which injure people every year.

When to go

Most park roads are open to regular cars from late spring until early fall, with the exact dates depending on snow. Summer brings the warmest weather and the biggest crowds. September and early October are quieter, with golden grass and good wildlife watching, though snow can arrive early. In winter, most roads close to cars. The road between the North Entrance at Gardiner and Cooke City usually stays open, and Old Faithful can be reached by guided snowcoach or snowmobile tours. Check current road status on the National Park Service site before you go.

How long to stay

Three to four days lets you see the geyser basins, the canyon and a wildlife valley without rushing. Add a day or two to include Grand Teton National Park, just to the south.

Where to stay

Lodges and campgrounds inside the park, such as the historic Old Faithful Inn, are the most convenient, but they book up far in advance. Gateway towns, including West Yellowstone, Gardiner and Jackson, have many more choices.

Getting there from Salt Lake City

West Yellowstone is about five hours north of Salt Lake City by car, through Idaho Falls. Renting a car is essential, since there is no public transportation around the park.

Memory laneThen and now

The first visitors came by stagecoach and train. In 1903 the Roosevelt Arch was built at the North Entrance, carved with the words "For the Benefit and Enjoyment of the People." In 1904 the Old Faithful Inn opened, a giant log lodge with a lobby several stories high, and it still welcomes guests today. In the mid-twentieth century, the family car took over, and for years bears begging for food along the roads were one of the park's best-known sights. Feeding them is now strictly forbidden, for the bears' sake as much as ours. Gray wolves, gone from the park for decades, were brought back in 1995, and watching them in the Lamar Valley has become a new tradition. Old Faithful, meanwhile, keeps doing exactly what it has always done.

Good to know

  • Stay on the boardwalks: the ground in thermal areas can be thin, and the water is scalding.
  • Entrance fees and passes: check current fees and pass options before you go. An annual America the Beautiful pass may cover your entry.
  • Fuel and food: distances inside the park are long. Fill up and pack snacks.
  • Signal: cell coverage is patchy. Download maps before you arrive.
