How to plan a national park trip without the stress

Reservations, park passes, lodging and timing: a checklist for planning a US national park trip.

Somewhere in a shoebox, many of us have a faded photo of ourselves as a kid in front of a park entrance sign, wearing a souvenir T-shirt with a ranger hat that was much too big. The national parks have been part of American family life for over a century. Planning a visit takes more homework than it used to, but the feeling when you drive through the gate is the same as it ever was.

1. Check reservation and permit rules

Each park sets its own rules, and they can change from year to year. Before you book anything else, check the park's official page on nps.gov for:

  • Timed-entry or vehicle reservations, which some busy parks require in peak season.
  • Hiking permits for popular trails, often issued by lottery.
  • Campground reservations, which often open months in advance and sell out quickly.

Most reservations are made through Recreation.gov. Make an account in advance, so you are ready the moment a booking window opens.

2. Buy the right pass

If you plan to visit three or more fee-charging parks in a year, an annual America the Beautiful pass usually works out cheaper than paying separate entrance fees. US seniors, military members, fourth graders and people with permanent disabilities may qualify for free or discounted passes. Fees and pass rules have changed recently, including for international visitors, so check the current prices on nps.gov.

3. Book lodging early

In-park lodges and campgrounds are the most convenient and sell out first. Gateway towns just outside the park entrances have more choice. For peak season, book three to six months ahead, or even earlier for in-park lodges.

4. Time your visit

  • Arrive before 8 a.m. to beat crowds and find parking.
  • Visit in shoulder season (spring and fall) for fewer people and milder weather.
  • Midweek visits are calmer than weekends and holidays.

5. Pack for the conditions

  • Carry more water than you think you need, especially in desert parks.
  • Download offline maps. Cell service is limited in most parks.
  • Bring layers. Mountain and canyon temperatures can swing a lot in one day.

6. Leave no trace

Stay on marked trails, pack out your trash, keep your distance from wildlife and never feed animals. Rangers can give you a ticket for approaching wildlife too closely.

Memory laneThen and now

In the 1950s and 1960s, the "See the USA" era of new highways and family station wagons brought millions of first-time visitors to the parks. People collected bumper stickers and patches, stamped postcards at the visitor center and listened to ranger campfire talks under the stars. Many of those traditions continue. Pick up a passport book at a visitor center and start collecting cancellation stamps. Junior Ranger badges are still free for kids, and plenty of grown-ups earn them too.
