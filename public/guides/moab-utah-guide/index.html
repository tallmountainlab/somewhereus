Moab, Utah: a base camp for arches, canyons and the Colorado River

Moab has a way of showing up in old family slides: a station wagon parked under a red cliff, kids in striped T-shirts standing in front of an arch, the Colorado River glinting below. Grandparents who drove here in the 1960s remember a dusty uranium town with one main street and a couple of motels. The town has grown and filled with bike shops and cafés, but the light on the red rock at sunset is exactly the color in those old pictures.

Moab sits in eastern Utah, right next to Arches National Park and a short drive from Canyonlands. It is the perfect base for a few days of hiking, scenic drives and river views.

Arches National Park

Just north of town, Arches has more than 2,000 natural stone arches. Highlights include Delicate Arch, the arch on Utah's license plates, reached by a hike of about three miles round trip; the Windows Section, with several big arches a short walk from the parking lot; and Landscape Arch on the Devils Garden trail. In recent years, the park has used a timed-entry reservation system in the busy season, so check the official park website before you go.

Canyonlands: Island in the Sky

About 40 minutes from town, the Island in the Sky district of Canyonlands sits on a high mesa above huge canyons carved by the Colorado and Green rivers. Walk the short trail to Mesa Arch at sunrise, when the underside of the arch glows orange, and stop at Grand View Point for one of the biggest views in the Southwest.

Dead Horse Point State Park

On the way to Island in the Sky, this state park looks down on a tight gooseneck bend of the Colorado River 2,000 feet below. It is especially beautiful at sunset. It is a Utah state park, so it charges its own entrance fee, and a national parks pass does not cover it.

Scenic Byway 128 and Fisher Towers

Northeast of town, Highway 128 follows the Colorado River through a red-walled canyon. It is one of the prettiest drives in Utah, with picnic spots and riverside campgrounds along the way. Turn off for Fisher Towers, a cluster of tall, dark red spires with a hiking trail weaving between them.

Corona Arch and Potash Road

West of town, Potash Road (Highway 279) runs along the river past rock art panels and dinosaur tracks. Nearby, the Corona Arch trail is a hike of roughly three miles round trip, with a short section of steps and handlines leading to a huge arch.

Getting there

From Salt Lake City, Moab is about four hours by car via U.S. 6 and I-70. Canyonlands Regional Airport, north of town, has limited flights, so most travelers drive. A car is essential.

When to go

Spring (March to May) and fall (September to October) are the favorite seasons, with warm days and cool nights, but they are also the busiest. Summer is very hot, so hike early in the morning and carry plenty of water. Winter is quiet and cold, and a dusting of snow on the arches is a special sight, though some trails can be icy.

Memory laneThen and now

Moab began as a small Mormon farming settlement in the late 1800s. In the 1950s, it became a boomtown almost overnight, after prospector Charlie Steen struck a rich uranium deposit south of town in 1952. Miners, families and new motels poured in. Around the same time, Hollywood discovered the red rock, and director John Ford filmed Westerns nearby, beginning with Wagon Master and Rio Grande in 1950. Arches, protected as a national monument since 1929, became a national park in 1971. Writer Edward Abbey worked there as a seasonal ranger in the 1950s and later described his time in Desert Solitaire, a book that made a generation of readers dream of the canyon country. Today the uranium mines are gone, and visitors come for the hiking, biking and silence instead.

Good to know

  • Water: carry at least one gallon per person per day in summer.
  • Stay on the trails: the dark crust on the sand is living soil that takes decades to recover.
  • Book ahead: lodging in Moab fills up on spring and fall weekends.
