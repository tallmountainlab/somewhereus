You have seen Monument Valley even if you have never been there. It was the backdrop to the old Westerns your grandparents watched on Saturday afternoons, the place John Wayne rode across on a horse, the view on a hundred car commercials and the road where Forrest Gump finally stopped running. Then you drive in for real, the sun drops, the great red buttes light up like coals, and you realize the movies never quite captured it.

Monument Valley in brief

Monument Valley sits on the Utah-Arizona border, inside the Navajo Nation. The main area is Monument Valley Navajo Tribal Park, run by the Navajo Nation, not the National Park Service. That matters for planning: the America the Beautiful pass is not accepted here, and the park charges its own entrance fee. Check current fees and hours on the official Navajo Parks and Recreation site before you go.

The Valley Drive

The classic way to see the park is the Valley Drive, a loop of about 17 miles on an unpaved road that winds among the buttes. You can drive it in your own vehicle. Most regular cars manage it in dry weather if you go slowly, but the road is sandy, bumpy and rutted in places, and it can be rough after rain. Some rental car agreements do not allow driving on unpaved roads, so read the fine print.

Along the way you will pass the Mittens, two buttes shaped like giant hands, along with Merrick Butte, the Three Sisters and John Ford's Point, the overlook named for the director who filmed here so often. Allow two to three hours with stops.

Guided tours

Large parts of the valley, including some arches and backcountry areas, can only be visited with a Navajo guide. A guided tour in an open-air truck, on horseback or on foot is the best way to go deeper and to hear the stories of the families who have lived here for generations.

Forrest Gump Point

North of the valley on U.S. Highway 163, near mile marker 13 on the Utah side, is the spot where the long, straight road runs toward the buttes, made famous in the film Forrest Gump. It is beautiful, but it is also a highway. Pull fully off the road, watch for traffic and never stand in the lane for a photo.

Where to stay

  • Inside the tribal park: The View Hotel looks straight out at the Mittens. Book well ahead.
  • Goulding's Lodge: just outside the park, with a small museum in the old trading post.
  • Kayenta, Arizona, and Mexican Hat or Bluff, Utah: more options, a short drive away.

Getting there

From Salt Lake City, plan on roughly six hours by car. A popular route heads south through Moab and the canyon country, which makes it easy to combine with Arches and Canyonlands. From the Grand Canyon's South Rim, it is about three hours.

Nearby stops

  • Goosenecks State Park: a tight bend of the San Juan River seen from a cliff edge, near Mexican Hat.
  • Valley of the Gods: a quieter, free gravel loop with its own sandstone towers.
  • Four Corners Monument: where Utah, Arizona, Colorado and New Mexico meet.

When to go

Spring and fall are the most comfortable seasons. Summer can be very hot, and afternoon thunderstorms are possible in late summer. Winter is cold and quiet, and a light dusting of snow on the red rock is a rare treat. Whatever the season, sunrise behind the Mittens and the late-afternoon glow are the best times for photos.

One tip: the Navajo Nation observes daylight saving time, while most of Arizona does not. Double-check what time it is when booking tours or sunrise trips.

Then and now

The Navajo, or Diné, have lived in and around these valleys for centuries, herding sheep and raising families among the buttes. In the 1920s, Harry Goulding and his wife Leone, nicknamed Mike, opened a trading post here. During the hard years of the Depression, so the story goes, Harry traveled to Hollywood with photographs of the valley, hoping to bring work to the area. Director John Ford came to film Stagecoach, released in 1939, and returned again and again for Westerns that made Monument Valley the image of the American West for millions of people around the world. The tribal park opened to visitors in 1958. Today the trading post is a museum, and the views are exactly as they were in those old black-and-white films.

Good to know

  • Respect the land: stay on the marked roads, do not climb the rock formations and ask before photographing people.
  • Alcohol: the Navajo Nation prohibits alcohol, so leave it out of the car.
  • Drones: not allowed without permission.
  • Fuel and water: distances are long. Fill up in Kayenta, Mexican Hat or Bluff and carry water.
