There is one photo of Grand Teton that hangs in more dentist offices, diners and grandparents' living rooms than you can count: a weathered wooden barn in a meadow, with jagged, snow-streaked peaks rising behind it. It looks like a painting, and the first time you stand in front of the real thing, it is hard to believe it is not. The Tetons do not have foothills. They shoot straight up from the valley floor, and that is what makes them unforgettable.

Grand Teton National Park sits in northwest Wyoming, just south of Yellowstone, above the town of Jackson. The highest peak, Grand Teton itself, rises to 13,775 feet.

Classic stops

Mormon Row

On the east side of the valley, a line of old homestead barns and cabins stands along a gravel road. The most photographed is the Moulton Barn, especially at sunrise when the peaks glow pink behind it.

Schwabacher Landing

A short gravel road leads down to the Snake River, where beaver ponds reflect the Tetons on calm mornings. It is a favorite spot for photographers at dawn.

Oxbow Bend

A slow curve of the Snake River with Mount Moran rising behind it. Bring binoculars: moose, elk, otters and birds such as pelicans are often seen here, especially early and late in the day.

Jenny Lake

A clear mountain lake at the foot of the peaks. A short boat shuttle crosses the lake to the trail for Hidden Falls and Inspiration Point, a rewarding hike with views back across the valley. You can also walk around the lake instead of taking the boat.

Chapel of the Transfiguration

A small log chapel built in 1925, with a window behind the altar that frames the Tetons. It is still used for services in summer.

Wildlife

Grand Teton is one of the best places in the lower 48 states to see moose. Look for them in willow flats along rivers. Bison, elk, pronghorn and black and grizzly bears also live in the park. Stay at least 100 yards from bears and wolves and 25 yards from other animals, and never feed wildlife.

Jackson

The town of Jackson, just south of the park, is famous for the elk-antler arches on its town square, wooden boardwalks and Western-style storefronts. In winter, sleigh rides take visitors among the thousands of elk that spend the season on the National Elk Refuge.

Getting there

Jackson Hole Airport is unusual: it sits inside the national park, with mountain views from the runway. From Salt Lake City, it is about five hours by car. Yellowstone's South Entrance is just north of Grand Teton, connected by the John D. Rockefeller Jr. Memorial Parkway, so it is easy to visit both on one trip.

When to go

Summer, from about June to early September, has the warmest weather, open roads and boat shuttles, and the most visitors. Late September brings golden aspens and the elk rut, when bulls bugle across the valley. Winter is quiet and snowy. Some park roads close to cars, but others stay open, and Jackson becomes a ski town. Check the National Park Service site for current road and facility openings.

Then and now

In the 1890s, Mormon settlers from Idaho came over the mountains and homesteaded the dry flats east of the Snake River, building the farms and barns of Mormon Row. Life was hard, with short summers and long, cold winters. When Grand Teton National Park was first created in 1929, it protected mostly the mountains. In the following years, John D. Rockefeller Jr. quietly bought up ranch land in the valley to protect the view, and after a long and heated local debate, much of that land was added to the park in 1950. Today the barns of Mormon Row are preserved, and the same sunrise that greeted the homesteaders still lights up the peaks every morning.

Good to know

  • Entrance fees: check current fees and pass options. An America the Beautiful pass may cover entry.
  • Bear spray: carry it on trails and know how to use it.
  • Lodging: in-park lodges and campgrounds book up fast, so reserve early.
