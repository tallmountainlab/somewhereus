Somewhere in an old family album there is a photo of a station wagon parked in front of a neon motel sign, kids squinting in the sun, a cooler on the tailgate. Maybe it was your parents, maybe your grandparents. Chances are it was taken on Route 66. The Mother Road turned 100 in 2026, and driving it is still the closest thing America has to a time machine: diners with chrome stools, gas stations turned into museums and long, empty stretches where the radio is the only company.

Route 66 in brief

U.S. Route 66 was established in 1926 and ran about 2,400 miles from Chicago to Santa Monica, through eight states: Illinois, Missouri, Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona and California. As interstate highways replaced it, the old road was gradually bypassed, and it was officially removed from the U.S. highway system in 1985. Much of the original road still exists as local highways and frontage roads, marked with brown "Historic Route 66" signs. Following it takes a little patience and a good map, and that is half the fun.

How long to take

To drive the whole road without rushing, plan on two to three weeks. You can cover it faster, but the point of Route 66 is to slow down, stop at the odd roadside attraction and eat pie at the counter. If you only have a week or less, pick one or two states and do them properly.

Best stops, east to west

Illinois and Missouri

  • The "Begin" sign in Chicago, near Grant Park, for your starting photo.
  • The Gemini Giant in Wilmington, Illinois, a towering fiberglass astronaut from the 1960s.
  • The Old Chain of Rocks Bridge near St. Louis, now a walking and cycling bridge with a famous bend in the middle.

Kansas and Oklahoma

  • Galena, Kansas, on the road's short Kansas stretch of only about 13 miles.
  • The Blue Whale of Catoosa, a smiling concrete whale beside a pond in Oklahoma, built in the early 1970s.
  • The Oklahoma Route 66 Museum in Clinton, a good overview of the road's history.

Texas and New Mexico

  • Cadillac Ranch near Amarillo, a row of half-buried Cadillacs from 1974 that visitors are invited to spray-paint.
  • The MidPoint Café in Adrian, Texas, roughly halfway between Chicago and Los Angeles.
  • The Blue Swallow Motel in Tucumcari, New Mexico, with its glowing neon sign and old-style garages beside each room.

Arizona

  • Petrified Forest National Park, the only national park the road passes through, with a rusting old car marking the alignment.
  • The Wigwam Motel in Holbrook, where you can sleep in a concrete teepee.
  • Williams, a neon-lit main street and the starting point for the Grand Canyon Railway.
  • Seligman, where local efforts to save the road began in the 1980s, and the long, quiet stretch west through Peach Springs and Hackberry.
  • Oatman, an old mining town where wild burros wander the main street.

California

  • Amboy and the Mojave Desert, home to Roy's Motel and Café sign, standing alone in a vast desert.
  • The Santa Monica Pier, with the "End of the Trail" sign by the Pacific.

Short on time? Try Arizona

If you can give the road only a few days, Arizona is a favorite choice. The stretch from Seligman west to Kingman is one of the longest unbroken pieces of the old highway, and Williams, Flagstaff and Holbrook are all close by. It also combines well with a visit to the Grand Canyon. From Salt Lake City, it is a long day's drive south, so you can fold it into a bigger southwest loop. See our Arizona road trip for ideas.

When to go

Late spring (May and June) and early fall (September and October) are the sweet spots. Summer brings extreme heat in the Mojave Desert and long days in the Plains, while winter can bring snow and ice to the high country around Flagstaff and in parts of New Mexico. Many small museums and family businesses keep shorter hours outside the summer season, so call ahead if a stop matters to you.

Then and now

In the 1930s, Route 66 carried families fleeing the Dust Bowl toward California, and John Steinbeck called it "the mother road" in The Grapes of Wrath. After the Second World War, it became the road to vacation: Bobby Troup's song "(Get Your Kicks on) Route 66," made famous by Nat King Cole in 1946, sent a generation west in new cars, past motor courts, trading posts and diners that competed with ever-bigger signs. When the interstates came, many of those towns were suddenly bypassed and went quiet. Since the late 1980s, local associations, collectors and small-business owners have brought neon signs back to life and reopened old motels. For the 2026 centennial, towns along the route have planned celebrations, so check local calendars as you plan.

Good to know

  • Navigation: phone maps prefer the interstate. Use a dedicated Route 66 guidebook, map or app to stay on the old alignments.
  • Fuel and water: gas stations can be far apart in the desert. Fill up when you can and carry water.
  • Road surface: some original sections are rough or unpaved. Check conditions before you take a side alignment.
  • Support the small places: eat at the independent diners and buy a souvenir at the family shops. They are the reason the road still exists.
